# 4 - Persuasion patterns and dark patterns as scam evidence

Question: scammers use persuasion tricks such as urgency, scarcity, authority and social proof. If a site uses them, is that a reliable warning sign even when no factual error is visible? How reliable is that evidence?

Short answer: **the bare presence of a persuasion pattern is weak evidence.** Legitimate, popular sites use these patterns a lot, and the best fraud-site detectors do not rely on them. What *is* useful is a pattern that can be **shown to be fake** in a way someone else can check: a timer that resets, a "12 people viewing" counter made by a random number generator, a low-stock count that changes on reload, a seal that does not verify, or social icons that link nowhere. Even then, it proves that the marketing is dishonest. It does not prove that the product does not exist.

Method: every source listed under "Verified" was opened (page or PDF text) on 2026-11-25. Page or section references come from the opened text. Numbers come from the papers. Most of them date from 2014 to 2025, so the current rates may differ. Check them against the source before quoting them publicly.

## What the research says, in one paragraph each

**Scam psychology (why people fall for it).** Stajano & Wilson derive seven principles from filmed street cons: Distraction, Social Compliance (authority), Herd (social proof), Dishonesty, Deception, Need and Greed, and Time (urgency). They state that three of Cialdini's principles map onto theirs: Social Proof to Herd, Authority to Social Compliance, and Scarcity to Time (TR-754, Section 4, near the end before "5 Conclusions"). The same passage notes that ordinary sales and marketing use the same techniques ("quasi-scams ... technically legal"). That is the base-rate problem in the authors' own words. The Exeter news page (2009) describes scams that use emotion (excitement, fear) and borrowed authority, and says that victims are "unduly open to persuasion" rather than poor decision-makers.

**Which cues actually move people.** In phishing email, van der Heijden & Allodi analysed more than 80,000 phishing emails from a European bank (Section 1). Scarcity and Consistency cues correlate positively with clicks. Social Proof, Authority and Liking show "no evident trend", and Reciprocity looks counterproductive (Figure 13, Section 5). Note that *all* their emails were phishing, so this study says which cues make a scam work better, **not** whether the cues separate scam from legitimate content. In the Luguri & Strahilevitz website experiment, a countdown-timer "scarcity" condition ("this offer will expire in the next 60 seconds") had **no significant effect**: 14.3% vs 14.8% control (Section 3.2.2, Table 3). Social proof raised acceptance modestly (22.1% vs 14.8%), and hidden information worked best (30.1%). In other words, the urgency that people assume scammers rely on was the *least* effective pattern in that experiment.

**How common the patterns are on legitimate sites (the base rate).**
- Mathur et al.: on about 11K *popular* shopping sites, 1,818 dark-pattern instances were found on 1,254 sites (about 11.1%). "Shopping websites that were more popular ... were more likely to feature dark patterns." This is a lower bound, because only text was analysed (p. 81:2). Per type (Table 1, p. 81:12): Countdown Timer 393 instances on 361 sites, Limited-time Message 88/84, Activity Message 313/264, Testimonials of unclear origin 12/12, Low-stock Message 632/581, High-demand Message 47/43.
- EU CPC sweep (IP/23/418): 148 of 399 retail web shops (about 37%) had at least one of three dark-pattern types, and **42 used fake countdown timers** (about 10.5%).
- Di Geronimo et al.: 95% of 240 popular Android apps contained at least one dark pattern, with at least seven types per app on average (abstract and results section).
- These are mostly *legitimate* businesses, many of them large. A judge that treats urgency or social proof as a fraud signal will therefore flag a large share of honest sites.

**How fraud detectors actually work.** BEYOND PHISH (6,127 crowdsourced fraudulent e-commerce sites) mentions that fraud sites use "limited time offers or showing recent purchases as notifications" (Section II-A). However, it does **not** use any persuasion cue as a classifier feature. It explicitly reports that discount size, once a classic fake-shop cue, no longer separates fraud from legitimate sites: "most of the recent FCWs do not offer significant discounts" (Section IV-E). Its features are infrastructure and checkable social presence (Table III). Its top features by SHAP score are valid social media links, likes, account age, sub-domain level, registration period and domain age (Table VIII, Section VI). Its strongest single content cue is a *checkable fake*: 81.47% of fraud sites had no social media link and 16.80% had an invalid one (logo without a link, or a link to someone else's page), compared with 33.85% of legitimate sites without social links (Section IV-E). It reports a 98.34% detection rate and a 1.34% false-positive rate on unseen data from a user study (abstract). SCAMMAGNIFIER (1,155,237 shopping domains, 46,746 fraudulent) mentions scarcity and discount "illusion" only as background (Section I). Its linking evidence is again checkable: fraud sites that share the same merchant ID, registrar, hosting and template (Sections I and III).

**Trust seals.** Van Goethem et al. examined ten security-seal providers and 8,302 sites that use seals. A seal that links to the provider's own page is harder to fake than a copied image (Section 2.1). However, **all ten providers' seals could be embedded on a phishing page** and still show the provider's "this site is secure" page (Section 5, Phishing). The seal is invisible rather than negative when a site fails the provider's scan. A visitor cannot tell "no seal" from "failed seal".

### B2B software sites vs consumer shops

- **Relevant for B2B and SaaS vendor sites:** fake or copied trust seals; dead or foreign social icons; "featured in" and customer-logo walls with nothing to back them; testimonials with stock photos or reused quotes; unverifiable certification badges; "offer ends tonight" or "only N licences / seats left" on pricing pages; evergreen countdowns on launch or webinar pages; shared merchant or analytics IDs.
- **Mostly consumer shops:** low-stock per product, "X people viewing this", "just bought" pop-ups, high-demand messages, and discount-based fake shops. The large measurements (Mathur, EU sweep, BEYOND PHISH, SCAMMAGNIFIER) were all done on **shopping** sites. **No verified study measures dark-pattern or persuasion-cue rates on B2B software vendor sites.** Any rate we assume for SaaS is a guess.

## How reliable is this?

1. **Presence of a persuasion pattern is not evidence of fraud.** Legitimate sites use urgency and social proof widely: 11.1% of popular shops (a lower bound, with *more* popular shops using *more* of them), 37% of shops in the EU sweep, and 95% of popular apps. No verified study shows these cues occurring at clearly higher rates on fraud sites using the same method. The leading fraud classifier works without them and found that the old "big discount" cue stopped working. Flagging on presence would produce many false alarms, and the people who wrote the research say so about their own grey areas (Mathur, Section 6.4).
2. **A pattern shown to be fake is real evidence, but of something narrower.** A reset timer, `Math.random()` "viewers", scheduled stock counts, a copied seal or a dead social link prove that the site **states falsehoods to sell**. That is a legitimate trust finding for the "is the company trustworthy?" question, and it can be checked by a third party (screenshots at two times, the script URL, the seal link). It does **not** show that the product is fake or dead: Mathur's 140 sites with fake timers were mostly real, popular shops.
3. **The strongest content signals are about verifiability, not persuasion.** Invalid social links, unverifiable seals and badges, and shared merchant IDs outperform "pressure" language.
4. **The evidence on what fools people is mixed.** Urgency had no measurable effect in the one controlled website experiment found (Luguri & Strahilevitz). In phishing, scarcity correlates with clicks, but that data contains no legitimate comparison group.
5. **Recommended weighting for the judge:**
   - Presence of a pattern: log it, weight 0.
   - Shown to be fake: one medium-to-strong "dishonest marketing" reason, with the proof link.
   - Never let persuasion findings alone decide whether the product is real.
   - Always state the false-alarm note next to the reason.
6. **Age and scope caveats.** The data runs from 2014 (seals) and 2019 (Mathur) to 2023-2025 (BEYOND PHISH, SCAMMAGNIFIER). Plugin vendors, platform policies such as Shopify's, and law (the EU DSA and the FTC 2024 rule) may have changed since. All the large measurements are on shopping sites, not B2B software.
