# 2 – How people and experts judge website credibility

Research basis for an agent that judges whether a software product is real and alive and whether its vendor is trustworthy.

The brief (opened, PDF p. 1, section "Looks are only half of it"): "Anything a vendor writes about itself is now free to produce. The signals that still mean something take time or need someone else: customers who name the vendor on their own sites, a domain older than last month, a team with a history, a press report the vendor didn't write." It also says: "An agent that believes everything fails. So does an agent that doubts everything."

Verification note. I opened every source listed as "verified" on 2026-11-25 and checked its title, authors and year. Page numbers are the numbers printed on the page unless marked "PDF p.". Anything I did not open is marked **not verified**.

## 1. B.J. Fogg and the Stanford Web Credibility Project (Stanford Persuasive Technology Lab)

**Who.** B.J. Fogg, Ph.D., Stanford Persuasive Technology Lab, with co-authors from Consumer WebWatch (Consumer Reports) and Sliced Bread Design.

**Why they count as experts.** This is the best-known large empirical programme on how ordinary users judge web credibility. The project's guidelines page says the guidelines are "based on three years of research that included over 4,500 people". Its publications page lists peer-reviewed CHI and DUX papers from 1999 to 2003. The report cover gives Fogg's affiliation as Stanford Persuasive Technology Lab.

**Sources (all verified):**
1. Fogg, B.J., Soohoo, C., Danielson, D., Marable, L., Stanford, J., Tauber, E.R. (2002). *How Do People Evaluate a Web Site's Credibility? Results from a Large Study.* Consumer WebWatch / Stanford PTL report, released 29 Oct 2002. https://advocacy.consumerreports.org/wp-content/uploads/2013/05/stanfordPTL.pdf (the peer-reviewed short version is DUX 2003 in the ACM Digital Library, paywalled, **not opened**)
2. Stanford, J., Tauber, E.R., Fogg, B.J., Marable, L. (Oct 2002). *Experts vs. Online Consumers: A Comparative Credibility Study of Health and Finance Web Sites.* Consumer WebWatch. https://advocacy.consumerreports.org/wp-content/uploads/2013/05/expert-vs-online-consumers.pdf
3. Fogg, B.J. (May 2002). *Stanford Guidelines for Web Credibility.* https://credibility.stanford.edu/guidelines/index.html (publication list: https://credibility.stanford.edu/publications.html)

**How to use it.** This line of research mainly describes what ordinary users react to, and those are exactly the cues a vendor can now produce for free. The guidelines are advice for site owners on how to *look* credible ("list a physical address", "design your site so it looks professional"). For our judge they describe what a fake will imitate. They are not evidence of trust.

## 2. Sam Wineburg, Sarah McGrew and the Stanford History Education Group (now Digital Inquiry Group)

**Who.** Sam Wineburg (Margaret Jacks Professor of Education, Emeritus, Stanford; founded the Stanford History Education Group (SHEG) in 2002; co-founder of the Digital Inquiry Group, DIG). Sarah McGrew (PhD Stanford, co-directed SHEG's Civic Online Reasoning project, now at the University of Maryland).

**Why they count as experts.** The DIG team page (https://inquirygroup.org/team) and history page (https://inquirygroup.org/history) confirm the roles above. They state that Wineburg's "current work focuses on how people judge the credibility of digital content". Their 2017/2019 study is the origin of "lateral reading", the method other researchers cite as the fact-checker strategy. For example, Kozyreva et al., source 2 below, PDF p. 11–12, cite it that way.

**Sources:**
1. Wineburg, S., McGrew, S. (2017). *Lateral Reading: Reading Less and Learning More When Evaluating Digital Information.* SHEG Working Paper No. 2017.A1, Sept 2017. **Verified.** PDF copy: https://www.hendrix.edu/uploadedFiles/Academics/Faculty_Resources/Teaching_and_Learning/EvaluatingDigitalInformation.pdf (SSRN: https://ssrn.com/abstract=3048994, not opened). It was published in 2019 as "Lateral Reading and the Nature of Expertise", *Teachers College Record* 121, with a repository record at https://purl.stanford.edu/yk133ht8603. I saw the record title, but I **did not compare the journal text** with the working paper. The page numbers below refer to the 2017 working paper.
2. Kozyreva, A., Wineburg, S., Lewandowsky, S., Hertwig, R. *Critical Ignoring as a Core Competence for Digital Citizens.* Preprint PDF in the Stanford repository (PDF metadata 2022): https://purl.stanford.edu/gk771kw4093 (file: https://stacks.stanford.edu/file/gk771kw4093/Critical%20Ignoring%20Online.pdf). **Verified (preprint).** The final journal version, which I believe is *Current Directions in Psychological Science*, 2023, is **not verified**. Note that the first author's institution is the Max Planck Institute for Human Development, Berlin.
3. Wineburg, Breakstone, McGrew, Smith, Ortega, *Lateral Reading on the Open Internet: A district-wide field study* (SSRN 3936112; *Journal of Educational Psychology*). **Full text not opened.** Its numbers (n = 271 treatment vs 228 control, six 50-minute lessons) are cited here only as reported in source 2, PDF p. 12.

## 3. Mike Caulfield (SIFT, *Web Literacy for Student Fact-Checkers*)

**Who.** Michael A. Caulfield, digital literacy researcher. His own site (https://hapgood.us/about/) currently gives his role as manager of Academic and Collaborative Technology at University of Washington Bothell. Earlier affiliations are **not verified** here.

**Why they count as experts.** He created SIFT ("Stop, Investigate the source, Find better coverage, Trace claims to the original context"). He co-wrote *Verified* (University of Chicago Press, 2023) with Wineburg. The book itself was **not opened**; the co-authorship is confirmed on the DIG team page. I confirmed his standing independently, not just from his own site: the DIG team page lists him as a DIG affiliate and "Creator of SIFT", and Wineburg & McGrew (2017, p. 46) cite him for the core idea of using the web's "web-like properties". His own About page makes larger claims about SIFT's reach. These are self-description and I did not confirm them.

**Sources (all verified):**
1. Caulfield, M.A. *Web Literacy for Student Fact-Checkers.* Open textbook, CC BY 4.0, 2017 according to secondary sources (the PDF itself is undated). PDF: https://hapgood.us/wp-content/uploads/2018/09/Web-Literacy-for-Student-Fact-Checkers-1543512063.pdf
2. Caulfield, M. (19 June 2019). *SIFT (The Four Moves).* https://hapgood.us/2019/06/19/sift-the-four-moves/
3. Caulfield, M. (23 Feb 2018). *Media Literacy Is About Where To Spend Your Trust. But You Have To Spend It Somewhere.* https://hapgood.us/2018/02/23/media-literacy-is-about-where-to-spend-your-trust-but-you-have-to-spend-it-somewhere/ (blog post, not peer reviewed; it is the source of the over-scepticism warning)

**Also relevant, not checked:** Miriam Metzger and Andrew Flanagin (UC Santa Barbara) on credibility heuristics: reputation, endorsement, consistency across sources, expectancy violation, persuasive intent. I did not open their papers, so they are **not verified** and not used below.

## Findings → rules for our judge

| # | Finding | Evidence (number, page/section) | Rule for our judge | Source |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Ordinary users judge mainly by looks | "Design look" appears in 46.1 % of 2,440 credibility comments from 2,684 participants. That is the top category of 18, ahead of information design/structure at 28.5 % (Abstract p. 4; table of contents) | Never score visual polish, layout or "looks professional" as a trust signal. At most it shows the vendor invested in a site, and that is now cheap | Fogg et al. 2002 |
| 2 | Experts weigh looks far less and reputation, sources and motive far more | Design look: finance consumers 54.6 % vs finance experts 16.4 %; health consumers 41.8 % vs health experts 7.6 %. Health experts relied most on the name reputation of the site, its operator or its affiliates (43.9 %), then on cited information sources (25.8 %) and company motive (22.7 %). Panel: 8 health and 7 finance experts (Key Findings p. 4; method section) | Rank reputation as shown by others, named sources and the vendor's motive above anything about appearance | Stanford, Tauber, Fogg, Marable 2002 |
| 3 | Experts are wary of flashiness | Health experts were "wary of sites that had a flashy design" (Experts report, PDF p. 22) | Treat marketing gloss with no matching outside evidence as a mild warning, not a plus | Stanford et al. 2002 |
| 4 | What users *say* matters differs from what they *use* | Users claimed privacy policies are vital, yet mentioned them in < 1 % of comments; identity of the site operator 8.8 %; sponsorship 2.3 % (Key findings p. 6) | Check the operator's identity on purpose, because humans skip it. A privacy policy page on its own proves nothing | Fogg et al. 2002 |
| 5 | "Looking good is being good" is a known human bias, made stronger by low motivation | Discussion "Why is Design Look so prominent?" p. 58: appearance acts as a peripheral cue (Elaboration Likelihood Model) | An AI judge has no motivation shortfall. It should always do the costly check (leave the site) and not stop at the cheap cue | Fogg et al. 2002 |
| 6 | The credibility guidelines tell owners how to *look* credible | Guidelines 2, 5, 6, 8: show a physical address, contact details, a professional design and recent updates (guidelines page) | Address, contact page, "updated recently" and staff bios are claims the vendor makes about itself. They count only once something outside the site confirms them, e.g. a company register entry or an independent mention of the people | Fogg 2002 guidelines |
| 7 | Smart non-experts fall for easily faked surface features | Historians and students were "taken in by" the name, logo, .org domain, layout and "scientific" look of a lobbying site (abstract p. 1; p. 16). 60 % of students judged the lobby site *more* reliable, and only 3 of 25 told the two sites apart (p. 18) | Logos, an official-sounding name, the TLD, references and a professional layout get **zero** weight on their own | Wineburg & McGrew 2017 |
| 8 | Fact checkers leave the site quickly and read *about* it elsewhere | All 10 fact checkers correctly picked the reliable site (mean conclusion score 2.0 vs historians 0.7, students 0.16; p. 12). One checker left the landing page after 8 s (p. 13). Checkers left landing pages after 26 s on average vs 2 min 5 s for the others (p. 26) | First step for every vendor: search for the vendor's name *outside* its domain (news, Wikipedia, registers, customer sites, critics) before reading its own pages in depth | Wineburg & McGrew 2017 |
| 9 | Lateral reading finds who is really behind a site, quickly | Every fact checker found the funder behind a front-group site, in 3 min 25 s on average; 6 of 10 historians found it, taking about 6 min; 40 % of students found it, taking about 7 min (p. 23). 80 % of students spent no time on who was behind the site (p. 28) | Make "who owns or runs this, according to others" a required field, with a link to a third-party page | Wineburg & McGrew 2017 |
| 10 | "About" pages give little information | A checker read a front group's About text and called it "profoundly not helpful" (p. 24). In the checklists discussion, About-page claims, .org, address and contact details "were taken with a grain of salt" (p. 45) | Self-description is neutral until confirmed elsewhere. Quote it as the vendor's claim, never as the evidence for a reason | Wineburg & McGrew 2017 |
| 11 | Checklists focus on features anyone can fake | "Long or short, checklists focus students on a website's most easily manipulated features" and "create a false sense of security" (p. 44) | Do not build the judge as an on-page checklist (has address? has team page? has testimonials?). Weight independent, outside evidence highest | Wineburg & McGrew 2017 |
| 12 | Click restraint: pick the source to open, not just the first hit | Fact checkers spent about 20 s reading search snippets and URLs before clicking (pp. 32, 42). "A searcher's first click is often destiny" (p. 42) | Prefer results the vendor does not control (other domains, known outlets, registers) over the top hit. Record which results were skipped and why | Wineburg & McGrew 2017 |
| 13 | Weak vs strong heuristics | "Using a domain designation as a proxy for trustworthiness" is a weak heuristic; lateral reading is a strong one (p. 41) | Never use the TLD (.io, .ai, .org) as a signal. Domain *age* is different: it takes time and is visible in WHOIS, see #15 | Wineburg & McGrew 2017 |
| 14 | Critical ignoring: deep reading of a source you should not trust wastes attention | "If lateral reading indicates that the site is untrustworthy, examining it directly wastes precious time and energy" (PDF p. 12). Fact checkers "only needed a few seconds" (PDF p. 12) | Run the outside check first. Do the deep feature audit of the vendor's own pages only when outside signals do not already settle the verdict | Kozyreva, Wineburg, Lewandowsky, Hertwig (preprint) |
| 15 | WHOIS owner data is usually hidden, but the registration date is not | Privacy services are "becoming the norm, dramatically reducing the usefulness of WHOIS searches", but the registration date stays visible; for an "established" paper a domain first registered a year ago "would be fairly odd" (ch. 18, pp. 94–95) | Use the domain creation date as a signal that takes time to fake. Flag a mismatch between a recent domain and claims like "trusted since 2010". Do not count hidden WHOIS ownership against a vendor, because it is the norm | Caulfield, *Web Literacy* |
| 16 | Use previous work and Wikipedia as a starting point for consensus | "Check for previous work" is the first move (p. 6). Wikipedia "is often the best source to get a consensus viewpoint" because of its sourcing rules, though it needs caution on contentious or fast-moving topics (ch. 6, p. 21) | Look up existing third-party coverage (Wikipedia, review sites, press) first and follow its citations. Link the cited source, not just the summary | Caulfield, *Web Literacy* |
| 17 | Judge a source by its incentives and track record, not by its bias | Choose "a publication that has strong incentives to get things right, as shown by both authorial intent and business model, reputational incentives, and history" (ch. 17, p. 88) | Weight outside evidence by how much the outside source has to lose by being wrong: an established outlet or a named customer counts for more than an anonymous review or an affiliate listicle | Caulfield, *Web Literacy* |
| 18 | SIFT: Stop, Investigate the source, Find better coverage, Trace to original context | Blog post, section "Investigate the source": take "sixty seconds to figure out where media is from". Trace claims back to the original source | For each claim such as "used by X" or "SOC 2 certified", follow it to the original: X's own site, the auditor's registry. Never cite the vendor's restatement as proof | Caulfield, SIFT 2019 |
| 19 | Match the depth of checking to the stakes | In "Stop": quick checks are "good enough" for low-stakes uses, and both depths "are equally useful" (SIFT post) | Use a fast first pass for an obvious case (well-known vendor). Spend more steps only on ambiguous or high-stakes cases, and report how deep the check went | Caulfield, SIFT 2019 |

## Warnings against over-scepticism

| # | Finding | Evidence | Rule for our judge | Source |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A | "Trust compression": treating very different sources as equally compromised is itself a failure | Students called the Mayo Clinic as biased as a supplements seller and dismissed a cited expert as "not great". "Our problem is not gullibility, but rather the gullibility of cynics". "If everything is compromised, then everything can be ignored" (blog post, main text) | The verdict must separate levels of trust. A vendor with strong independent signals gets a clear "trustworthy", not a hedged "some concerns" because of one minor blemish | Caulfield 2018 blog |
| B | One past problem does not cancel a solid record | Students rated the National Post "half-and-half" because of one corrected 2006 error; Caulfield uses this as an example of poor judgment (same post) | Weigh a past incident (breach, outage, lawsuit) against how the vendor handled it and its overall record. Report it with a link, but do not let it decide the verdict automatically. Issuing a public correction counts *for* a source (*Web Literacy*, ch. 17, p. 86: reputable outlets "issue corrections when they get facts wrong") | Caulfield 2018; *Web Literacy* |
| C | Historians equivocated; their failure was calling both sites reliable or not deciding | Historians "often equivocated, expressing the belief that both sites were reliable" (p. 12). The rubric gave 1 point for "vague or indecisive" and 0 for wrong (p. 11). Fact checkers "unreservedly" chose the right site (p. 12) | Commit to a verdict when the evidence supports one. Use "unclear" only when outside evidence really is missing, and say which check would settle it | Wineburg & McGrew 2017 |
| D | Looks and outside reality often disagree in both directions | Brief examples: a dated site from a vendor with decades of customers. Experts in the 2002 study cared little about design, so a plain site was no strike against it (#2) | A dated design is *not* a negative signal. Judge age and liveliness from release notes, a changelog, repository activity or outside mentions | Brief; Stanford et al. 2002 |
| E | Starting points like Wikipedia are useful, not suspect by default | Wikipedia is "often the best source to get a consensus viewpoint" (*Web Literacy* p. 21). Fact checkers used it as their first step (Wineburg & McGrew p. 14) | Do not reject an independent summary just because it is crowd-edited. Use it and follow its references | Caulfield; Wineburg & McGrew |

## Caveats

- The Fogg data are from 2002, with self-reported comments from low-motivation participants (the authors say so, p. 58). The percentages describe what people *mention*, not measured causal effects. Use them as direction, not as exact weights.
- Wineburg & McGrew is a small sample (45 people: 10 fact checkers, 10 historians, 25 students) on social and political sites, not software vendors. Transferring its methods to vendor checks is our inference.
- I used the 2017 working paper; the 2019 journal version may differ in wording and pages.
- The Caulfield trust post is an essay, not a study.
- **Not verified:** the field-study full text (cited only through Kozyreva et al.), the final journal version of "Critical Ignoring", the DUX 2003 paper, *Verified* (2023), and Metzger/Flanagin.
